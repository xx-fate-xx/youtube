In 1880s Paris, thousands of clocks could not keep time on their own. Once a minute, they breathed. A puff of compressed air came up a thin lead pipe. It inflated a small rubber bellows. The bellows lifted a lever. A hooked pawl on the lever nudged a sixty-tooth wheel ahead by one tooth. Stops limited its swing: one puff, exactly one minute. Sixty puffs, one turn of the minute hand. Then the air was let out, and the lever dropped back to wait. Every breath came from one central station, through iron mains in the sewers. By late 1881, more than four thousand clocks in central Paris ran on air. Until one night in 1910, when a flood stopped them all. That story is on our channel.
