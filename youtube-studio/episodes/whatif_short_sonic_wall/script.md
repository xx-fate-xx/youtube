# Short 2 — "Hitting a Wall at the Speed of Sound"  (9:16, ~45 s)

Parent: whatif_super_speed.

VO: Your bones are a hundred times stronger than normal. You run into a concrete wall at the speed of sound.

VO: Your bones survive. Your organs don't.

VO: Stopping from about seven hundred and seventy miles an hour in about a foot and a half means roughly twelve
thousand g.

VO: Your skeleton stops. Your heart, your liver and your brain are soft, and they keep going, straight into your
unbreakable ribs and skull.

VO: In 1954, Air Force doctor John Stapp rode a rocket sled from over six hundred miles an hour to a dead
stop. About forty-six g, and he recovered with no lasting injury. Even the worst crash anyone is known to have survived peaked at
about two hundred g.

VO: Twelve thousand is in a different universe. Strong bones aren't enough.

VO: So what if every part of you was unbreakable? Full video, linked below.

VISUAL (vertical): hero sprinting toward a wall (city bg crop), freeze one metre before impact; chalk x-ray
mode: skeleton lines in white, organs as soft amber shapes that keep sliding forward; "≈ 12,000 g" vs "46 g"
and "≈ 200 g" bar comparison; rocket-sled stick figure silhouette (no real likeness of Stapp, labelled "1954 rocket sled").

Calc: a = v² / (2d) = 343² / (2 × 0.5) = 117,650 m/s² ≈ 12,000 g.

Facts: Stapp, Sonic Wind No. 1, 10 Dec 1954, 632 mph (≈ 1,017 km/h), 46.2 g (Smithsonian NASM). Highest
known survived (accidental): Kenny Bräck, 214 g peak, 2003 (Guinness). Deliberate higher: Beeding 83 g, 1958.
