## 0. Cold open — "Thirty floors"  (~0:00)

VO: A window cleaner slips off the thirtieth floor. That's about three hundred feet. Four and a half seconds
later, they hit the ground at around eighty-five miles an hour.

VO: But today, you're down there. You can run as fast as you want, and nothing can hurt you. You get there in
time. You hold out your arms. You catch them. And they almost certainly don't survive.

VO: Because that's the catch with being a superhero. You're unbreakable. The people you save are not. So let's
take your super speed out on a real shift, one rescue at a time, and see what saving someone actually takes.
And whenever the world freezes like this, we're stopping the clock to look at the physics.

VISUAL: tower facade, window-cleaning cradle; figure slips; you sprint in from the street. Freeze just before
the catch: chalk "300 ft", "≈ 85 mph · 135 km/h". Title "What if you had super speed?". Mission card:
"RESCUE 1 · THE FALL".

## 1. The fall  (~0:40)

VO: Let's rewind. The problem isn't how fast they're falling. It's how suddenly that speed disappears. Catch
someone in your arms and they stop in about a foot and a half. That's around a hundred and forty g.

VO: For comparison, John Paul Stapp, the Air Force doctor who rode rocket sleds to find out what a crash does to
a body, once stopped from more than six hundred miles an hour at over forty g. He was strapped in, and he
still came out bruised, with his vision gone for several minutes.

VO: So you have to stop them gently. The biggest roller coasters peak at around four to six g, and only for a
moment. To keep a falling person under about four g, you need almost sixty feet to slow them down. Which means
you can't wait at the bottom.

VO: So here's the move. Let's give you super grip as well: your shoes hold on any surface. You sprint straight
up the wall, meet them on the way down, and match their speed, so for a moment you're both falling together and
nothing hits anything. Then you brake, gently, over the last sixty feet.

VO: Lesson one: a rescue isn't about getting there fast. It's about stopping slowly.

VISUAL: rewind effect (motion runs backwards, chalk "REWIND"). Freeze: chalk "1.5 ft → ≈ 140 g" vs
"60 ft → ≈ 4 g"; small rocket-sled silhouette, "Stapp, 1954: > 40 g". You run up the facade (vertical camera
travel), match the faller, brake; amber "g-meter" stays under 4.

## 2. The burning tower  (~2:00)

VO: Next call: a fire, and people trapped on the thirtieth floor. In most deaths from house fires, it isn't the
flames that kill. It's the smoke. Fire safety experts warn that once the smoke alarm goes off, you may have less
than two minutes to get out of a burning home.

VO: So you run in, grab the first person, and head for the stairs. And immediately you hit a wall. Not
literally. The stairwell turns back on itself every floor, around a landing only about four feet wide. Swing
someone around a turn that tight at highway speed and the sideways force crushes them against your arms. To
keep it down to about two g, you have to slow to around ten miles an hour on every single turn. With a
passenger, your super speed is now a jog.

VO: So you take the fastest way down there is: the window. You step out holding them, run down the wall and let
them drop almost freely for two hundred feet, then brake at two g for the last hundred. About five seconds, start
to finish, like the big drop on a roller coaster. Then you're back up the wall alone, where nothing slows you
down.

VO: Five seconds down, a moment back up: around ten people a minute. Carry one under each arm and it's twenty.
Fifty people on that floor? Out in about two and a half minutes.

VO: Lesson two: with someone in your arms, your limit isn't your speed. It's how hard their body can be turned,
pushed and stopped.

VISUAL: tower interior, smoke layer under the ceiling (no flames on people). Freeze on the landing: chalk
"4 ft", turn arrow, "highway speed → crush", "≈ 10 mph · 16 km/h at 2 g". Exit through the window, run down the
facade; counter "people out: 1 … 50" with a clock.

## 3. The runaway truck  (~3:30)

VO: Down on the highway, a fully loaded truck has lost its brakes. Eighty thousand pounds, at sixty-five miles
an hour. Even with working brakes, a truck like that needs more than five hundred feet to stop.

VO: You step in front of it. You can't be hurt, so this is fine, right? Well, unbreakable doesn't mean heavy.
You still weigh about as much as an average man, around two hundred pounds. With ordinary shoes, you can push on
the road with roughly your own body weight, and the truck is four hundred times heavier than that. It would
shove you along the road for more than ten miles before it finally stopped.

VO: With super grip, you can push as hard as you like. But now think about the driver. Stop the truck dead in
three feet and everyone inside goes through nearly fifty g. That's a worse crash than the tests cars are
designed for, which hit a concrete wall at up to thirty-five miles an hour.

VO: So the heroic move is the boring one. You grab the frame and brake over about three hundred feet, roughly
what a car needs in an emergency stop. Inside the cab, it feels like slamming on the brakes. Nothing worse.

VO: Lesson three: being unbreakable doesn't make you immovable. And stopping too hard is its own kind of crash.

VISUAL: highway (NEW bg), truck as a simple painted-style vector; you skid backwards with sparks from the
soles. Freeze: chalk "80,000 lb vs 200 lb", "> 10 miles"; then "3 ft → ≈ 48 g" vs "300 ft → ≈ 0.9 g".

## 4. The bullet  (~4:50)

VO: Downtown, someone fires a gun. A typical pistol bullet leaves the barrel at about twelve hundred feet per
second, a little faster than sound. From thirty feet away, it gets where it's going in about a fortieth of a
second.

VO: And here super speed hits a different kind of wall. Your legs might be fast, but your brain isn't. A simple
reaction, like pressing a button when a light comes on, takes most people around a fifth of a second. That's
eight times longer than the bullet needs. By the time you notice the shot, it's already over.

VO: That's why comic-book speedsters always get a second power for free: a brain that runs fast too. So let's
give you that. Now the world slows to a crawl, and you can see the bullet coming. What do you do?

VO: Shove the person out of the way? Moving someone two feet in a fortieth of a second takes a push of nearly
two hundred g, about four times what Stapp went through. Step in front of it? You're unbreakable, so the bullet
hits you like a steel plate, and ricochets and fragments off hard surfaces are a real danger to anyone standing
nearby.

VO: The safest move is the strangest one. You run alongside the bullet, match its speed, and simply close your
hand around it. You'll be going faster than sound for a moment, so everyone on the street hears a bang. But
nobody gets hurt.

VO: Lesson four: speed is useless without a brain to match it.

VISUAL: street, night (night variant bg). Freeze: chalk "1,200 ft/s · 360 m/s", "30 ft in 1/40 s"; reaction
bar "you: 1/5 s" vs "bullet: 1/40 s". Slow-motion world (desaturated, ghosts) while you move. Bullet as a tiny
amber streak; no blood, no victim shown hit.

## 5. The wave  (~6:20)

VO: Our last call is the biggest, and this one really happened. On the twenty-sixth of December 2004, a
magnitude nine point one earthquake struck off the coast of Sumatra and sent a tsunami across the Indian Ocean.
In deep water, a tsunami can move as fast as a jet plane, more than five hundred miles an hour. It reached
Thailand around one and a half to two hours later, and Sri Lanka two to three hours later. At the time, the only
tsunami warning system of its kind was in the Pacific. About two hundred and twenty-eight thousand people were
killed or never found.

VO: Could you outrun it? Not on land. You'd have to run across the sea, the way a basilisk lizard does, slapping
the surface so hard and fast that the water can't get out of the way. Scientists studying these lizards worked
out that a human would have to slap the water at more than sixty-five miles an hour, with about fifteen times
more power than our muscles can produce. For you, that's easy.

VO: At a thousand miles an hour, you could cross the Bay of Bengal to Sri Lanka in about an hour, well ahead of
the wave. And then you'd hit the real limit. The coasts in the wave's path stretch for thousands of miles, and
you're one person. Even the warning is a problem: running past faster than sound, you'd arrive before your own
voice. All people would hear is your sonic boom, a sound NASA describes as thunder-like. Nobody runs for high
ground because of thunder.

VO: The real fix turned out to be less heroic, and far more powerful. After 2004, the countries around the
Indian Ocean built a tsunami warning system together. A first version was running by mid-2006, and by 2011 the
region was running it with its own warning centres: sensors, national alert networks, and warnings that can
reach a whole coastline at once. No runner could ever do that.

VISUAL: calm, respectful palette; ocean map (painted), wave front as a soft white arc, no depicted victims.
Freeze: chalk "> 500 mph · 800 km/h"; lizard silhouette "slap > 65 mph · 30 m/s, 15× muscle power". You running
on water with amber spray. Map ends with many small warning icons lighting up along the coast at once.

## 6. Ending — "They're not"  (~8:00)

VO: So here's what super speed really teaches you. The hard part of saving someone is never getting there. It's
everything after: stopping them gently, turning them softly, thinking fast enough, and reaching everyone at
once.

VO: You're unbreakable. They're not. And the best superhero would never forget it.

VO: Which rescue would you try first? Tell me in the comments.

## calc_notes (our calculations; standard formulas, inputs sourced in factcheck_report.md)

- Fall: h = 30 storeys ≈ 95 m (≈ 3.2 m/storey; 300 ft). Quadratic drag, terminal vt = 55 m/s (belly-down,
  ~200 km/h, FAI): v = vt·√(1 − e^(−2gh/vt²)) = 37.3 m/s = 83 mph ≈ 134 km/h ("around 85 mph");
  t = (vt/g)·arccosh(e^(gh/vt²)) = 4.6 s ("four and a half seconds").
- Catch in arms: a = v²/(2d); d = 0.46 m (1.5 ft) → 37.3²/0.92 = 1,512 m/s² = 154 g; d = 0.5 m → 142 g
  ("around 140 g"). To stay ≤ 4 g: d = 37.3²/(2·4·9.81) = 17.7 m = 58 ft ("almost sixty feet").
- Stairwell turn: landing ≥ 44 in (1.12 m) → centre-line radius ≈ 1 m. v = √(a·r): 2 g → 4.4 m/s = 9.9 mph
  ("around ten mph"). Highway speed 65 mph = 29 m/s at r = 1 m → 86 g lateral.
- Window descent: 300 ft; accelerate ~1 g for 200 ft (61 m) → v = √(2·9.81·61) = 34.6 m/s (77 mph), t = 3.5 s;
  brake 2 g: d = 34.6²/(2·19.6) = 30.5 m = 100 ft, t = 1.8 s; total 5.3 s ("about five seconds").
  10 people/min assumes ~6 s per round trip; 2 at a time → 20/min; 50 people → 2.5 min.
- Truck: 80,000 lb = 36.3 t; 65 mph = 29.1 m/s. Your push with shoe friction μ ≈ 1 → F ≈ your weight
  (200 lb ≈ 91 kg); 80,000/200 = 400. a = g/400 = 0.0245 m/s² → d = 29.1²/(2·0.0245) = 17.2 km = 10.7 miles
  (μ = 0.65 → 16 miles) ("more than ten miles"). Stop in 3 ft (0.91 m): a = 29.1²/1.83 = 463 m/s² = 47 g
  ("nearly fifty g"). Stop in 300 ft (91 m): a = 4.63 m/s² = 0.47 g. Note: a car's 316 ft from 65 mph is
  ≈ 0.45 g average including reaction distance; "feels like slamming on the brakes" holds.
- Bullet: 1,180 ft/s (9 mm, 115 gr); sound 1,125 ft/s at 20 °C. 30 ft / 1,180 ft/s = 0.025 s ("a fortieth of
  a second"). Reaction 0.2 s / 0.025 s = 8. Shove 2 ft (0.61 m) from rest in 0.025 s: a = 2d/t² =
  1.22/0.000646 = 1,890 m/s² = 193 g ("nearly 200 g"); 193/46.2 = 4.2 ("about four times Stapp").
- Water slap: > 30 m/s = 67 mph ("more than sixty-five mph"); ~15× human muscle power (Minetti 2012 citing
  Glasheen & McMahon 1996).
- Tsunami: epicentre → Sri Lanka east coast ≈ 1,655 km (great-circle estimate, USGS epicentre) ≈ 1,030 miles;
  at 1,000 mph ≈ 1 h; wave arrived 2–3 h after the quake (USGS).
