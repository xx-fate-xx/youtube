# What If You Had Super Speed? (And Nothing Could Hurt You)

Channel: new 4th channel, working title "What If" (stickman explainer, English). Format: long, 16:9, ~8:30.
Premise: the viewer's stickman ("you", amber scarf) gets super speed AND an unbreakable body. Each level shows
which rules of physics still apply. Companion Shorts (body stays human / organs / corners) link here.

Every number below is computed in `calc_notes` or taken from a source in factcheck_report.md. Narration is
original; ideas list from the operator was used only as a starting point.

Visual grammar: painted background per location (Higgsfield), mocap stickmen drawn in code, level HUD (top
left), "freeze-frame physics" (world desaturates, chalk notes) at the key moment of each level, scarf = speed.

---

## 0. Cold open — "One new ability"  (~0:00)

VO: You wake up with one new ability. You can run as fast as you want. And to keep things simple, your body is
unbreakable: bones, skin, organs, all of it. Nothing can hurt you.

VO: Sounds like the perfect superpower. But physics still has rules, and they show up a lot sooner than you'd
think. So let's speed up, one level at a time. And whenever the world freezes like this, we're stopping the
clock to look at the physics.

VISUAL: apartment interior, morning (NEW bg) → hero stretches, steps out → city street (have). Title card
"What if you had super speed?". HUD appears: LEVEL 0 · 5 km/h.

## 1. Level 1 — 44 km/h, Usain Bolt  (~0:30)

VO: Level one: forty-four kilometres an hour. That's Usain Bolt at his fastest, about twelve metres every second,
measured during his world record in Berlin in 2009.

VO: The wind in your face is what forecasters call a strong breeze. Nothing dramatic. But here's the surprise:
the fastest sprinters don't win by moving their legs faster. Studies of runners show that top speed comes
from hitting the ground harder. For the split second a foot is down, it pushes with several times the
runner's body weight. Speed, it turns out, is mostly about force.

VISUAL: city street, hero sprints; freeze: chalk arrow under the foot "several × body weight".

## 2. Level 2 — 130 km/h, motorway  (~1:20)

VO: Level two: a hundred and thirty, motorway speed. The air pushes back like a category one hurricane, and
your unbreakable body shrugs that off. The real trouble is your brain.

VO: Even the fastest human reactions take about a quarter of a second, and for a surprise it's closer to a full
second. At this speed that's nine to thirty-six metres before you even start to react. A cat steps off the
kerb, and you are already past it.

VO: And then there's grip. To turn, your shoes push sideways against the road, and they can only push as hard
as friction allows. Rubber on dry concrete gives you a bit less than one g. So the tightest curve you can follow
at this speed has a radius of about a hundred and seventy metres. The street corner you take every day? You'd sail
straight past it.

VISUAL: suburban road (NEW bg). Freeze: chalk curve with r ≈ 166 m vs. a street corner; "9 m" marker for reaction.

## 3. Level 3 — 1,235 km/h, the speed of sound  (~2:30)

VO: Level three: one thousand two hundred and thirty-five kilometres an hour. The speed of sound.

VO: That's about twenty-eight times faster than Bolt. But the air doesn't push back twenty-eight times harder.
Air resistance grows with the square of your speed, so it pushes back roughly seven hundred and sixty times
harder. Against your chest that's more than three tonnes, about the weight of two mid-size cars, shoving you
backwards.

VO: You're unbreakable, so you're fine. Everything around you isn't. Moving faster than sound, you drag a shock
wave behind you: a sonic boom. For half a century, the United States banned civil supersonic flight over land
because of booms from jets flying more than ten kilometres up. You'd be making yours at street level, a few
metres from people's windows.

VO: You'd also outrun your own voice. Sound can't travel ahead of something moving faster than sound, so the
people in front of you hear nothing at all, until you've passed them and the boom arrives all at once.

VO: Actually, getting here has a catch. Your feet can only push on the road as hard as friction allows, a bit
less than your own body weight with good rubber soles. Somewhere around a hundred and seventy kilometres an hour, the air
is pushing back just as hard. Past that point, ordinary shoes simply slip. To reach this level at all, you'd
need super grip as well, so let's give you that too.

VO: Stopping, on the other hand, is easy. Stop pushing, and the air slams on the brakes for you, at more than
forty g.

VO: And then there's fuel. Even if every calorie you ate turned straight into motion, one minute at this speed
would burn around a hundred and sixty thousand kilocalories. That's about eighty days of food. Per minute.

VISUAL: long straight road / salt flat (NEW bg) → city street (have). Freeze: arrow "AIR", "≈ 28× speed →
≈ 760× drag", "> 3 t". Unfreeze: sonic boom over the street, windows rattle, bystanders flinch. Chalk
runway 7.5 km. Food counter: plates stacking to "80 days".

## 4. Level 4 — 3,600 km/h, almost Mach 3  (~4:00)

VO: Level four: three thousand six hundred kilometres an hour, almost three times the speed of sound. The air
now pushes back with around thirty tonnes, probably more. But the bigger problem is heat.

VO: The air can't get out of your way fast enough. It piles up in front of you and gets squeezed, and squeezed air
heats up. Right at the front of your body it can reach around five hundred degrees Celsius, as hot as a
wood-fired pizza oven.

VO: The SR-71 Blackbird, a spy plane that cruised at about this speed, was built almost entirely from titanium,
because aluminium would have been weakened by the heat. Your skin can take it. Your clothes can't: cotton
ignites at around two hundred and fifty degrees. If the wind hadn't already torn them off back at level three,
the heat would finish the job. In comics, speedsters always keep their outfits. In real physics, they'd be
running naked.

VISUAL: desert highway (NEW bg). Freeze: chalk "air squeezed → ≈ 500 °C"; scarf glows and frays into sparks
(amber); clothing silhouette burns away to the plain stick figure (gag, no nudity issue — stickman).

## 5. Level 5 — 28,400 km/h, orbital speed  (~5:10)

VO: Level five: twenty-eight thousand four hundred kilometres an hour. And here something strange happens.

VO: The Earth is round, so as you run, the ground curves away beneath you. Normally gravity pulls you back down
long before that matters. But at about seven point nine kilometres per second, the ground curves away exactly as
fast as you fall. Your feet leave the road and don't come back. You're not running any more. You're in orbit,
and you'd circle the whole planet in about eighty-five minutes.

VO: Except you're still inside the atmosphere, so you're also a meteor. Spacecraft returning at this speed face
temperatures well over a thousand degrees. Behind you, a streak of glowing plasma. Ahead of you, the ocean.

VISUAL: coast (have) → sky above clouds (NEW bg) → orbit (have). Chalk: curved Earth, "fall = curve",
7.9 km/s. Hero with amber plasma trail.

## 6. Level 6 — the speed of light  (~6:20)

VO: Final level: the speed of light. Here physics simply says no. Anything with mass, and that includes you,
would need an infinite amount of energy to get there.

VO: Just the energy to get you to ninety-nine percent of it would be roughly what all of humanity uses in three
and a half weeks. But say you have it. You can get close, just not here. Anything moving that fast through air would smash its molecules with
the energy of a particle accelerator and wreck everything around it, so this level happens in space.

VO: At ninety-nine percent of light speed, time itself stretches. Run for what feels like one year, and about
seven years pass for everyone back home. The Moon would be a bit more than a second away.

VO: And even space isn't safe at that speed. It isn't completely empty: there's roughly one atom of gas in every
cubic centimetre, often less. At nearly the speed of light, each one hits you like a particle from an
accelerator, and together they'd be a lethal storm of radiation. Researchers who ran the numbers concluded
that real starships shouldn't go much faster than half the speed of light.

VISUAL: orbit (have) → deep space (NEW bg). Chalk: clocks "1 year (you) / 7 years (Earth)". Atoms as tiny
amber streaks.

## 7. Ending — what super speed really costs  (~7:30)

VO: So here's the strange truth about super speed. The speed itself never hurts. Right now, the Earth is carrying
you around the Sun at about thirty kilometres every second, and you don't feel a thing.

VO: What gets you is everything you touch on the way: the ground you push against, the air you push through, and
the people standing next to you when the boom arrives. At motorway speed it's your reflexes and the corners. At
the speed of sound it's the air and the noise. At three times that, heat. Faster still, the planet lets go of you,
and near light speed even empty space fights back.

VO: Which level would you stop at? Tell me in the comments.

VISUAL: meadow (have), hero sits on the hill at dusk; recap of the six level badges; end screen.
