Friday night, January 1910. Within minutes, the clocks of central Paris stopped. Not the ordinary ones. Only the clocks that ran on air. For nearly thirty years, a puff of compressed air had pushed their hands forward every minute. All that air came from one plant on the quai de la Gare, right on the Seine. And the river was rising, about a metre a day. That evening, the water got into the plant. No compressors, no pressure. Between ten forty-eight and ten fifty-three that night, the hands froze. The Seine kept climbing for a week, and some twenty thousand buildings flooded. The air came back on the third of February, after thirteen days of silence. How a city ran its clocks on air is on our channel.
