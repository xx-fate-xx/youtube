On August 16, 1858, Queen Victoria sends a message across the Atlantic. It's addressed to President James Buchanan, and it runs to 98 words. With stops and repeats, getting it through takes about sixteen hours. On average, that's roughly one word every ten minutes. And still, New York goes wild. The city celebrates for days with parades, church bells and fireworks. The fireworks even set the roof of City Hall on fire. Tiffany & Co. buys leftover cable and sells short pieces as souvenirs. Then the signals coming out of the cable start to fade. By early September, the line is effectively dead. Two summers of work went into laying that line. So why did it fail within a month? And why did anyone agree to try again? In the 1850s, news from London reached New York by steamship. The crossing took around ten days. A trader in New York priced his cotton on news that was already ten days old. Governments sent instructions to their ambassadors knowing events could change completely before the ship arrived. On land, the problem had already been solved. By the 1850s, telegraph wires linked most big cities in Britain and in the United States. Nobody had managed to wire across the ocean, close to 2,000 miles of water between Ireland and Newfoundland. In 1854 a wealthy New York paper merchant named Cyrus Field got pulled into a plan to run a telegraph line across Newfoundland. Looking at a globe in his study, he asked a bigger question. If the wire could reach Newfoundland, why stop at the coast? He raised money on both sides of the Atlantic, and in 1856 the Atlantic Telegraph Company was founded in London. One of its directors was a 32-year-old physics professor from Glasgow named William Thomson. Thomson had done the math on long cables, and it worried him. Copper wire, wrapped in insulation and lying in seawater, soaks up electric charge along its whole length. Every pulse you send has to fill that line before anything shows up at the far end. A crisp dot leaves Ireland and reaches Newfoundland as a slow, smeared bump. In 1855 Thomson calculated that this delay grows with the square of the cable's length. Make a cable ten times longer and the delay gets roughly a hundred times worse. The company's chief electrician was Wildman Whitehouse, a surgeon by training who argued in public that Thomson was wrong. Whitehouse believed a weak signal could be cured by pushing more voltage into the line. The directors wanted the cable laid in 1857, so they rushed a thin cable that Whitehouse backed. The cable they ordered was only about as thick as your finger. At its center ran seven copper wires twisted together. Around them went three coats of gutta-percha, a hard, rubbery gum taken from trees in Southeast Asia. Then came tarred hemp, and on the outside, 18 strands of twisted iron wire. Each mile of it weighed about a ton. Two English firms armored about 2,500 miles of it, one in Greenwich and one in Birkenhead. Later someone noticed the two halves had their iron armor twisted in opposite directions. Joining them took a specially made splice. The cable was divided between two warships, HMS Agamemnon from Britain and USS Niagara from the United States. In August 1857 the Niagara set out from Valentia Bay, paying cable over its stern as it steamed west. After hundreds of miles of cable, over water two miles deep, the brake on the paying-out gear was applied too hard and the cable snapped and sank. That ended the 1857 attempt, and the ships sailed home to wait for summer. For 1858 the engineers changed the plan. Both ships would meet in mid-Atlantic, splice their two halves together, and then steam away from each other toward opposite coasts. In June 1858 a storm hit the fleet on the way out. The Agamemnon, top-heavy with cable, rolled so hard that coils broke loose and her crew feared she would capsize. Once the weather cleared, the cable broke three times and the ships returned to Ireland. Some directors wanted to quit and sell what was left. Field talked them into one more try that same summer. On July 29, 1858, back in mid-ocean, the two ships spliced the cable for the last time. Seven days later, on August 5, both ships reached land. Agamemnon brought her end ashore at Valentia, Niagara brought hers ashore at Trinity Bay, Newfoundland. Trouble showed up within days of the landing. The pulses arriving at the far end were so weak that Whitehouse's relay receivers barely worked. What worked was a device Thomson had designed, the mirror galvanometer. A tiny magnet with a mirror on its back hung from a single silk thread. A lamp shone onto that mirror and bounced a spot of light onto a paper scale. The faintest current twisted the magnet a little, and the light spot jumped left or right. One swing meant a dot, the other a dash. With it, operators could read messages one slow letter at a time. At first, Whitehouse had used his own equipment, with huge induction coils that hit the line with thousands of volts. Somewhere under the Atlantic, the already-flawed insulation gave way. Seawater reached the copper, current leaked into the ocean, and the signal faded day after day. By early September, no readable message got through. Whitehouse, dismissed back in August, took the blame. Some newspapers even suggested the whole thing had been a hoax. In 1859 the British Board of Trade and the company set up a joint committee to find out why cables failed. It spent about two years testing cables and taking evidence, and in 1861 it published a long report on how to build, test and lay them. Thomson's approach won out, including testing all the copper for conductivity. Then the American Civil War began in 1861, and the project stalled for years. By 1865 the company had a new cable and a new ship. The Great Eastern, designed by Isambard Kingdom Brunel, was the largest ship afloat at the time. She could hold the whole Atlantic cable in one trip, so there was no need for a mid-ocean splice. In July 1865 she left Ireland, and after more than 1,000 miles the cable snapped and dropped into water over two miles deep. The crew dragged the seabed with grappling hooks for days and raised the cable three times, but each time the line broke. In 1866 the Great Eastern sailed again with fresh cable. On July 27 she landed it at Heart's Content, Newfoundland. This time the line held. Then the crew went back to the spot where the 1865 cable lay on the bottom. They hooked it, hauled it up from more than two miles down, spliced on fresh cable and finished the line to Newfoundland. By September 1866 there were two working cables across the Atlantic. The new line moved about eight words a minute. At its best, the 1858 cable managed a word or two a minute. News that once spent ten days on a steamship could now reach the other side in minutes. William Thomson was knighted for his part in the work. Years later he became Lord Kelvin, and the kelvin, the scientific unit of temperature, carries his name. By 1870 a chain of cables ran from Britain to Bombay. In 1871 another line came ashore at Darwin, in northern Australia. Modern cables on the seabed carry pulses of light through glass fibers. The first transatlantic fiber cable, TAT-8, went into service in 1988. Estimates commonly put the share of data crossing between continents through undersea cables at around 99 percent. The station at Heart's Content stayed in use until 1965. The building is now a museum, and much of its old cable equipment is still in place. When you load a page hosted on another continent, the data most likely crosses the ocean floor, along the kind of route those ships first proved could work in 1866.
