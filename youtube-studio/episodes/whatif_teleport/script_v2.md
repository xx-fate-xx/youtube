# What If You Could Teleport? (Your Speed Comes Too)

Draft v1 (2026-11-23), written to whatif-narration-rules + .claude/skills/make-episode/retention.md: one number
per scene said as an image, no jargon, stakes and humour; hook in 3 beats; open loop (the one place this power is
great) paid off at the end; soft CTA at ~25 %; re-hook at ~60 %; no "thanks for watching".
Facts: research_notes.md (every number sourced or computed there). Narration in US units; chalk shows both.
Rule of the power (on screen): you keep your velocity **as seen from space** (Earth-centred, not spinning) —
Option A in research_notes.md; Option B is mentioned once and never used.
Target ≈ 1,250 words ≈ 8:20 at ~2.5 words/s.

---

## 0. Cold open — "It's a door, not a brake"  (~0:00)

VO: You can teleport. Anywhere, instantly. And the first person you try to save still hits the ground.

VO: A window cleaner slips off a skyscraper, about thirty floors up. You blink them down to the street. Safe. Except they arrive still falling, as fast as a car on the highway.

VO: Because this power has one rule. You can change where you are. You can't change how fast you're moving. Your speed comes with you.

VO: And that one rule turns a trip to the North Pole into a car crash, a vacation into a cannon launch, and the whole spinning planet into a trap. But there's one secret route where this power works perfectly. Stay for that one.

VISUAL: tower_plate; cleaner slips (existing sprites); hero snaps fingers → amber teleport ring; cleaner pops out
of a ring at street level still in the falling pose, slams down (dust, no gore); freeze: chalk "SAVED?" → "NOPE".
Rule card on screen: "RULE: your speed comes with you". Teaser montage (1 s each): skid on ice, launch into sky,
spinning globe, rocket on a launch pad with "?".

## 1. The window cleaner  (~0:35)

VO: Let's rewind. The trick is timing. Teleport them in the first half-second, and they land with a stumble. Wait just one second, and they're already falling as fast as a bike rolling downhill.

VO: Wait until the end, and they're doing over eighty miles an hour. Teleporting them to the street doesn't save them. It just picks where they crash.

VO: And sending them back up to the roof? They'd arrive still diving at full speed, straight into the floor up there. Same crash, nicer view.

VO: Same with you. Your car skids toward a wall, and you teleport out to the sidewalk. You land on the sidewalk at exactly the speed the car was doing. Congratulations. You just crashed without the car, the seatbelt or the airbag.

VO: Lesson one: a teleporter is a door, not a brake.

VO: Although, it is a very good accelerator. Open one portal on the floor and another on the ceiling, and jump in. You fall, come out at the top, and fall again, faster every lap, until the wind holds you at about a hundred and twenty miles an hour. Free speed, forever. That's a perpetual motion machine, and physics says no. A real teleporter would have to charge you for every bit of height you gain. Nobody gets free energy.

VISUAL: rewind; HUD "FALL SPEED" counting up; freeze at 0.5 s ("stumble ✓") and at 1 s with a chalk bike rolling downhill; freeze at the
bottom: chalk car, "82 mph · 132 km/h"; roof gag: ring on the rooftop, cleaner pops out head-first, crash dust,
amber "nicer view ✓". Car gag: highway_plate, a car (existing truck plate? simple code-drawn car) skids,
hero blinks out and tumbles along the sidewalk; chalk "same speed, no car". Lesson card. Portal loop: two amber rings
(floor/ceiling) in a code-drawn room or the stairs_plate; hero loops with speed lines, HUD "FALL SPEED" caps at
"≈ 120 mph"; chalk "free energy? PHYSICS SAYS NO".

## 2. The ground isn't still  (~1:25)

VO: Okay, you're careful now. You're on a beach in Ecuador, right on the equator, and you want to see the North Pole. Nothing's falling. Nothing's moving. Right?

VO: Wrong. You're standing on a spinning planet. Every day, the equator goes all the way around, so the sand under your feet is moving faster than sound. You never feel it, because the sea, the air and you are all moving together.

VO: Now teleport. The North Pole doesn't travel anywhere. It just turns on the spot, like the middle of a spinning record. So you arrive with your speed, and the ice doesn't have any.

VO: You don't fall. You skid, sideways, faster than a typical pistol bullet.

VO: That skid carries the crash energy of about a dozen cars hitting a wall at highway speed. All of it, in you.

VO: Quick note for the physics fans: we're measuring your speed the way it looks from space. If it were measured against the ground you just left, nothing would happen at all. Which would make this a very short video.

VO: If this is breaking your brain a little, subscribe. It gets worse.

VISUAL: NEW beach_plate (equator beach, Ecuador-ish coast, a painted equator line marker on the sand); hero
relaxed; camera pulls out to a code-drawn globe seen from above the North Pole, spinning, arrows of different
lengths ("equator: fast", "pole: 0"); chalk "≈ 1,040 mph · 1,670 km/h" small; NEW pole_plate (sea ice, flat
horizon); hero pops out of a ring and skids across the ice with sparks of ice spray, tumbling (NEW skid pose);
chalk "faster than a typical pistol bullet"; chalk row of 12 tiny cars → wall. Frame note card: "speed as seen from space".
Soft CTA: small amber "SUBSCRIBE · it gets worse" chalk, 6 s, while the hero digs himself out of a snow bank.

## 3. Go east, get launched  (~3:05)

VO: It gets worse the farther you go. Back on the equator in Ecuador, you teleport a quarter of the way around the world, east, into a rainforest in Africa.

VO: Picture Earth from above. In Ecuador, your speed pointed along the ground. A quarter of the way around, that same direction points straight up.

VO: So you arrive, and the planet throws you into the sky like a cannonball, while the jungle slides out from under you.

VO: Go a quarter of the way west instead, and the same speed points straight down. You arrive by drilling into the Pacific Ocean.

VO: And the far side of the planet is the worst of all. There, the ground spins the other way, so you hit it at twice the speed.

VISUAL: globe from above with the hero's velocity arrow staying fixed while the globe-point moves a quarter turn
→ the arrow now points outward (chalk "same arrow, new 'up'"); NEW jungle_plate; hero shot upward out of the
ring (NEW launched pose) with leaves and a vapour cone; ocean_plate: hero drills in head-first, big splash column;
antipode: globe with two opposite arrows, chalk "× 2".

## 4. The safe zone  (~4:15)

VO: So is teleporting useless? Not at all. It's all about distance. The farther you jump, the bigger the mismatch can get.

VO: Jump one mile, and you won't even notice. Jump a hundred miles, and you land like you fell off a fast bike. Jump a thousand, and you could be done.

VO: Your commute is safe. Your vacation is not.

VO: So, no more vacations? Not so fast. There's a secret route, and we'll get to it. But first, here's what else is waiting at the other end of a jump.

VISUAL: map_plate with three chalk circles around a city (1 mi / 100 mi / 1,000 mi) and three tiny landing
doodles (✓, bike crash, ✗); "commute ✓ / vacation ✗" punch; Re-hook at ~60 %: "there's a secret route — we'll get to it" → montage.

## 5. Other ways a jump goes wrong  (~5:20)

VO: Teleport up to where airliners fly, and the air is so thin you've got maybe half a minute before you stop thinking straight.

VO: Teleport to the bottom of the deepest ocean trench, and every fingernail-sized patch of your body carries the weight of a small car.

VO: Teleport into a room, and the air that was already in your spot has to get out of the way, all at once. Every jump comes with a bang, like a gunshot. Two, actually. One where you arrive, and one where you left.

VO: And the Moon? It's racing around the Earth, and you're not. You'd arrive out of step with it, slamming into the dust much faster than a pistol bullet.

VISUAL: quick montage, a new location every ~4 s: NEW sky_plate (above the clouds, an airliner far away; hero
yawning, frost; chalk "≈ 30–60 s"); NEW trench_plate (dark sea floor, a lamp beam; chalk car balanced on a
fingernail doodle); a living room (NEW room_plate) — two chalk "BANG" bursts; NEW moon_plate — hero skids across
the lunar dust in a long trail, chalk arrows.

## 6. The secret route  (~6:20)

VO: So where does this power finally work for you? Here's the secret route. Every place on Earth has a twin on the other side of the equator: the same distance south, on the same line down the map.

VO: The planet spins like a wheel on an axle, so your twin is moving in exactly the same direction, at exactly the same speed, as you are.

VO: From New York, the twin spot is just off the coast of southern Chile, more than five thousand miles away. Teleport there, and you arrive perfectly still. Perfectly still, and a little bit wet.

VO: Rocket engineers love the spin too. It's why Europe launches its big rockets from French Guiana, close to the equator, aimed east. The planet hands each one about a thousand miles an hour before the engines even light. Your teleporter can't add a single mile an hour. It can only choose where your speed is pointing.

VO: So the trick was never to fight your speed. It's to land somewhere that's already moving just like you.

VISUAL: globe (code-drawn) with New York and its twin linked by a chalk line straight down the map, two identical
arrows; hero pops out of a ring just off the Chilean coast (ocean_plate reuse, coast at the right), stands
waist-deep, perfectly still, shrugs (a6) — chalk "0 mph ✓ · a bit wet"; NEW launch_plate (coastal launch pad near
the equator, generic rocket, no logos) with chalk "free ≈ 1,000 mph · 460 m/s, aimed east"; ring vs rocket
"teleporter: + 0 mph".

## 7. Ending  (~7:35)

VO: So teleporting doesn't let you skip physics. Your speed always comes with you, and on a spinning planet, everything is already moving.

VO: Jump small. Jump smart. And never, ever teleport to the North Pole.

VO: If you could teleport, knowing the rules, where would you go first? Tell me in the comments.

VISUAL: recap freezes (chalk only) — door-not-brake, skid, cannonball, twin spots; hero at the beach at dusk,
snaps fingers, ring opens, hero hesitates and steps back. End screen.

## calc_notes (from research_notes.md; all rounded, "about")

- Fall: v(t) = vt·tanh(g t / vt), vt = 55 m/s (≈ 120 mph, Davis, Body Physics §8.4 "Physical Model for Terminal Velocity", 90 kg flat posture "120 MPH"). t = 1 s → 35 km/h (22 mph,
  "a bike rolling downhill"); 300 ft (91 m) → t ≈ 4.5 s, 132 km/h = 82 mph ("over eighty", "a car on the highway").
- Car gag: OUR REASONING (velocity kept); no number. Portal loop: terminal velocity ≈ 120 mph (Davis §8.04, belly-down);
  energy conservation → a real teleporter must pay m·g·Δh per lap (OUR REASONING).
- Equator ground speed: ω·R = 7.292e-5 × 6,378 km = 465 m/s = 1,674 km/h = 1,040 mph (NASA Earth fact sheet);
  speed of sound ≈ 761 mph at sea level (NASA Glenn) → "faster than sound" (≈ 1.4×).
- Pistol bullet ≈ 1,180 ft/s = 805 mph (Federal 9 mm 115 gr, parent factcheck row 19) < 1,040 mph → "faster than
  a pistol bullet".
- Energy: ½ · 70 kg · 465² = 7.6 MJ; car 1,500 kg at 100 km/h (62 mph) = 0.58 MJ → ≈ 13 cars; at 70 mph ≈ 10 →
  "about a dozen" (car mass assumed typical). Chalk: "≈ 12 cars at highway speed".
- Quarter turn: inertial velocity fixed; at 90° east the old "east" is the local "up" (and the local ground moves
  465 m/s west relative to you): relative velocity ≈ 465 m/s up, and you move 465 m/s west relative to the ground (the ground slides east under you). 90° west →
  straight down (+ west).
  Antipode: relative 930 m/s = "twice the speed".
- Ecuador beach ≈ 80° W → +90° ≈ 10° E on the equator: inland Gabon (rainforest) ✓; −90° ≈ 170° W: open Pacific ✓
  (fact-check 2026-11-23).
- Distance rule: mismatch ≤ ω × distance: 1 mi → 0.26 mph; 100 mi → 26 mph ("fell off a fast bike"); 1,000 mi →
  263 mph ("you're done"). Upper bounds.
- Mirror latitude: v = ω ẑ × r = ω(−y, x, 0) does not depend on z → (φ, λ) and (−φ, λ) move identically.
  New York 40.7° N 74.0° W → 40.7° S 74.0° W ≈ 15 km off the coast of southern Chile (Caleta Cóndor 40.77° S
  73.84° W) → "just off the coast", "a little bit wet"; distance ≈ 5,600 mi ("more than five thousand").
- Airliner altitude 35,000 ft: time of useful consciousness 30–60 s (FAA PHAK Ch. 16, Fig. 16-1) → "maybe half a
  minute".
- Challenger Deep ≈ 10.9 km (NOAA NCEI / Greenaway 2021); +1 atm per 10.06 m (NOAA) → ≈ 1,090 atm ≈ 1.1 t per cm²
  ("a small car on a fingernail" — car ≈ 1 t, generic).
- Thunderclap: OUR REASONING (70 L of air displaced instantly), no numbers on screen.
- Moon: orbital speed 1.022 km/s (NASA Moon fact sheet); mismatch ≈ 1.0 km/s ± 0.47 km/s (Earth's spin) =
  0.56–1.49 km/s — below lunar escape speed 2.38 km/s, so you are NOT flung into space (research note corrected):
  you hit / skid at 1.5–4× a pistol bullet (0.36 km/s). Direction partly vertical in general — "skidding" simplified.
- Teleporting never changes your speed as seen from space; it only re-aims it relative to the ground (the old
  "free push to orbit" payoff was physically backwards — removed after fact-check).
- Kourou 5°3′ N; Earth's rotation "increases the speed of a launcher by 460 m per second" (ESA) = 1,029 mph
  ("about a thousand miles an hour"). Orbit ≈ 5 mi/s (NASA ISS facts) → ≈ 6 % head start.
