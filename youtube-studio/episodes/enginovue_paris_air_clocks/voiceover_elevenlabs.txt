On the night of Friday, the 21st of January 1910, the clocks of Paris began to stop. Not all of them. Only the ones that breathed. Between ten forty-eight and ten fifty-three that evening, every pneumatic clock in the city stood still, on lamp posts, in hotel lobbies, in offices and in private apartments. For nearly thirty years their hands had been moved by puffs of compressed air, sent through pipes under the streets from one central plant. That night, the flooded river Seine had reached the air plant on the quai de la Gare. No air, no time. But the clocks were only the beginning. The same company went on to build a far bigger network that carried power to thousands of customers, and it kept running until 1994. Every mechanical clock drifts. A few seconds a day turn into minutes within a month. Each one had to be checked and set by hand. Railways and the telegraph made those minutes matter. Cities wanted one shared time, kept by one master clock and copied everywhere else. Electric time signals already existed. But in Vienna, a different idea was tried: air. A Viennese telegraph engineer, Carl Albert Mayrhofer, patented clocks driven by pulses of air. With Victor Popp and the clockmaker Ernst Resch, the system went into service in Vienna in February 1877. Street clocks fed by a pipe all moved forward together: at first every two minutes, later every minute. Vienna was not convinced. After about three and a half years, the clocks were judged too inaccurate. Popp took the idea to Paris. At the 1878 World's Fair in Paris, the air clocks won a silver medal. That November, the city allowed a trial: pipes laid in the sewers, and clocks on street lamps. On the 15th of March 1880, the public service opened. Its heart was a building at 7 rue Sainte-Anne, in the centre of Paris. Compressors in the basement; above them, the master clock and a shop where anyone could sign up. A clock cost five centimes a day, and less for a second or a third. Hotels loved it: the Hôtel Meurice alone had a hundred and forty-eight of them. By late 1881, more than four thousand clocks were connected in seven hundred and fifty buildings. In 1881 the city granted a fifty-year concession to distribute time. Not a monopoly, but the right to use its streets and sewers. Paris now had a heartbeat. Once a minute, a puff of air went out across central Paris. Inside rue Sainte-Anne, a steam engine drove the air compressors. They filled a storage tank of about eight cubic metres at five atmospheres. A regulator let it into a second tank at just seven tenths of an atmosphere. The master clock had two separate movements. The left one was a precise pendulum clock that kept the time. The right one had a single job: moving the valve. At the sixtieth second of every minute, the valve opened the city's mains to the air tank. For twenty seconds, air pushed into the pipes. Then the valve flipped, and for forty seconds the pipes were vented to the open air. Twenty seconds on, forty off. Every minute, day and night. The clock even wound itself. Every minute, air pistons lifted its weights by exactly as much as they had fallen. A spare clock and valve stood ready, and alarms rang if a pulse failed. At first, once a day, the clock was compared with the Paris Observatory, and the few seconds it had gained or lost were corrected by hand. From rue Sainte-Anne, ten separate mains spread out under the streets. They were wrought-iron pipes just twenty-seven millimetres wide, laid in the sewers. If one main failed, the others kept working. At each building, lead pipes took over: fifteen millimetres in the stairwell, six in each apartment, and finally a tube of just three millimetres to the clock. How fast does a puff of air travel down a pipe? The front of the pressure wave moves at roughly the speed of sound, about three hundred and forty metres per second. That is about three seconds for every kilometre. A clock five kilometres away could not move until at least fifteen seconds after the valve opened. That is one reason the valve stayed open for twenty seconds: the far ends needed time to fill. In 1881, engineers found that the public clocks agreed with each other within about four seconds. Not perfect, but far better than clocks set one by one by hand. Leaks were unavoidable. But a leak only weakened the clocks beyond it, and twenty seconds of pressure was enough to drive clocks kilometres away despite the losses. The clock at the end of the line was simple. Inside sat a small bellows made of soft rubber. When the pulse arrived, the bellows swelled and pushed a rod upward. The rod raised a lever that carried a small hooked pawl. The pawl pushed a wheel with sixty teeth forward by exactly one tooth. Stops limited the lever's travel, so each breath moved the wheel just one tooth. A second catch held the wheel so it could not slip back. When the pipes were vented, the bellows sank and the lever dropped back, ready for the next minute. One pulse, one tooth, one minute. Sixty pulses, one turn of the minute hand. A small set of gears turned the hour hand. A burst bellows or a missed pulse left a clock behind until someone came to reset it. Clocks that struck the hours had a second bellows to wind their striking spring. But air could do far more than move a minute hand. In 1886 Victor Popp signed a treaty with Paris to deliver motive power straight to customers' premises. The company's plant on rue Saint-Fargeau, in Belleville, sent air at five atmospheres into a cast-iron main thirty centimetres wide. By 1889, at peak hours, its engines produced well over two thousand horsepower. That year the British engineer Alexander Kennedy came to test it. A list he was given showed two hundred and twenty-five installations, nearly all motors from half a horsepower to fifty. Lathes and saws, bookbinders, umbrella makers, the sewing machines of tailors, coffee roasters, confectioners, and the printing presses of Le Figaro. Some motors turned dynamos and made electric light. And expanding air turns cold: one restaurant piped the exhaust into its beer cellar and froze carafes of water. Air was not free energy. Much of the engines' work was lost as heat when the air was squeezed, and more when it expanded cold in the motor. Kennedy found that heating the air in a small coke stove just before the motor meant a motor four miles away got about ten horsepower for every twenty spent at the plant, counting the coke. Without heating, it took thirty-five. In 1892 a much larger plant opened on the quai de la Gare, beside the Seine. Four steam engines of eighteen hundred horsepower each drove its compressors. The clock company had become an energy company: by 1900 it had more than a hundred and sixty kilometres of power mains. And by then, it was selling electricity as well. By 1900, almost all of its money came from it. Which brings us back to January 1910. After heavy rain, the Seine was rising by about a metre a day. This was not a flash flood. It crept up, day after day. On the evening of the 21st, the water got into the compressed-air plant on the quai de la Gare. The compressors stopped, and the pipes lost their pressure. And between ten forty-eight and ten fifty-three, the pneumatic clocks of Paris stopped, all within a few minutes. Lifts and workshop motors driven by air stopped too. The river kept rising for a week. It peaked on the 28th of January at eight point six two metres on the Austerlitz gauge. Around twenty thousand buildings were flooded, and most metro lines were flooded too. The air clocks came back on the 3rd of February, after thirteen days of silence. First, the water had to be purged from the pipes. Paris was keeping time again. But the clocks were never much of a business. In 1880 each dial earned the company about seventeen francs a year and cost it about sixty. The number of private clocks peaked around 1884 at about seventy-eight hundred, then slowly fell. By 1926 about five thousand were left, with fewer than a thousand subscribers. Radio time signals were spreading, and electric clocks now offered a simpler alternative. On the 1st of March 1927, the company's licence for the clock service ran out, and it chose not to renew it. Its customers went to an electric clock firm, Magneta, and the city removed the pipes. After forty-seven years, the time of Paris no longer travelled by air. The air itself kept flowing. Only the clocks went quiet. The power network kept growing: five hundred and twenty-five kilometres of mains in 1930, and nine hundred and forty-one by 1970. It lifted elevators in the elegant buildings of western Paris, drove jackhammers on building sites and tools in garages and print shops. Its air sales peaked in 1973. After that, industry moved out of the city, and the last air-driven lifts disappeared. In 1994 the city ended the concession, and the public supply of compressed air across Paris came to an end. Part of the great plant on the former quai de la Gare still stands. Its metal hall and brick chimney are a listed monument. Today it is part of a school of architecture. For forty-seven years, part of Paris told the time by breathing. And on one flooded night in 1910, the whole city held its breath.
